Preface: The Hubris of the CTF Author


There is a special category of technical humiliation reserved for hardware security enthusiasts.


It doesn't happen when an adversary hacks your server. It doesn't happen when a bug hunter finds an IDOR you overlooked. It happens when you spend an entire week designing a ruthless, military-grade hardware challenge for a CTF competition—and then, in a moment of sheer post-challenge exhaustion, you lock yourself out of your own development board with your own security features.


Yes. I am that dummy.


Here is the story of how my innocent \$3 ESP32-S3 Super Mini transformed into an unyielding cryptographic fortress that refused to boot any normal firmware, how I stared into the silicon abyss thinking I had created a permanent high-tech keychain, and how a quirky parity-counting trick hidden inside the Espressif Technical Reference Manual gave me one final get-out-of-jail-free card.


1. The Crime: Designing "XTS-02"


It all started innocently enough. For the V1T CTF 2026 competition, I decided to author a real-world hardware challenge involving the ESP32-S3's hardware Flash Encryption engine (XTS-AES-128).


The architecture was beautiful:


  1. An ESP-IDF firmware built with CONFIG_SECURE_FLASH_ENC_ENABLED=y in Development mode.
  2. A provisioning Python script (tools/provision.py) designed to burn custom vendor keys into BLOCK_KEY0 and cryptographic metadata into BLOCK_USR_DATA.
  3. Players were supposed to analyze physical dumps, reconstruct partition tables, reverse key derivation routines, and bypass the hardware encryption layer.


To verify that the challenge was completely realistic and reproducible, I pulled my favorite daily-driver dev board from my desk: a tiny, purple ESP32-S3 Super Mini (QFN56, Silicon v0.2, 4MB Flash, native USB-C).


I connected it to COM10, flashed the firmware, ran the provisioning script with --commit, and smiled as the board booted.



The flag was verified. The challenge was packaged. High fives all around. I was a cryptographic mastermind.


Or so I thought.


2. The Next Morning: "Wait, Why Won't It Blink?"


Fast forward 24 hours. The CTF prep was finished, and I needed my little ESP32-S3 board back for a completely ordinary side project—just a simple Arduino sketch to test some sensors over USB Serial.


I opened VSCode, hit upload, and watched the flasher write the new unencrypted application binary.


Then I opened the serial monitor.


Silence.


I hit the RST button.


Instead of my friendly Hello World loop, the serial console spewed an endless torrent of register panics, illegal instruction exceptions, and infinite watchdog reboots:



I rubbed my eyes. “Okay, no problem, maybe the partition table is scrambled. Let’s just wipe it clean.”


I ran the universal embedded medicine:



It erased. I reflashed a clean bootloader. I reflashed the blink sketch.


Panic. Bootloop. Crash.


At that moment, the cold sweat hit me.


Flash memory had been erased, but the eFuses had not. And unlike flash memory, eFuses are One-Time Programmable (OTP) physical microscopic fuses etched into the silicon. Once blown, they are gone forever.


My obedient ESP32-S3 bootloader had done exactly what I told it to do the day before:


  1. It burned hardware Flash Encryption into the chip.
  2. It permanently disabled JTAG debugging (DIS_PAD_JTAG = 1, DIS_USB_JTAG = 1).
  3. It write-protected the internal AES key block.


Because Flash Encryption was active at the hardware level, every time the CPU tried to fetch an instruction from flash, the internal hardware bus ran it through the XTS-AES decryption engine.


When I uploaded an ordinary, unencrypted Arduino binary, the hardware dutifully "decrypted" my plaintext code into random mathematical garbage! The CPU tried to execute that garbage, choked on an illegal instruction, and crashed. Over. And over. And over.


I had effectively turned my only development board into a hardened paperweight.

Rule #1 of hardware triage: When you think you've bricked a chip, stop typing random commands. Do not randomly pass --force. Do not burn random fuses hoping for a miracle.

But look closely at the last line:



Why on earth is an on/off encryption switch a 3-bit field (0b001) instead of a single 1-bit boolean?


4. The Epiphany: Espressif's Brilliant Parity Trick


Here is where the genius of embedded hardware engineers saved my clumsy hacker soul.


In physical silicon, eFuses have a fundamental limitation: you can only burn a 0 into a 1. You can never, ever turn a 1 back into a 0. If Flash Encryption were governed by a single bit (0 = OFF, 1 = ON), the very first time you tested encryption on a dev board, that board would be permanently locked into encryption mode until the end of time.


To prevent developers from creating millions of accidental electronic paperweights, Espressif introduced a clever mechanism for Development Mode: Bit-Count Parity.


The chip doesn't check if the counter is non-zero. Instead, the hardware bootloader counts the number of set bits (1s) in SPI_BOOT_CRYPT_CNT:



Look at that state table:


  • 0 bits set (0b000 = 0): Even count → Flash Encryption DISABLED (Factory state).
  • 1 bit set (0b001 = 1): Odd count → Flash Encryption ENABLED (Where my dumb self was stuck).
  • 2 bits set (0b011 = 3): Even count → Flash Encryption DISABLED 🎉
  • 3 bits set (0b111 = 7): Odd count → Flash Encryption ENABLED (Game over, all fuses spent).


Do you see the miracle?


Because my board was only at 0b001, I still had two unburned zeroes in that register! And since the register was not write-protected, I could intentionally burn one more bit, transitioning the value from 0b001 to 0b011 (decimal 3).


Two bits set means an EVEN number of bits. The hardware interprets an even count as: DISABLE FLASH ENCRYPTION.


The hardware engineers literally gave us one built-in "undo" button.

6. What Did We Learn? (Besides Being a Dummy)


Looking back at this mini-crisis, I came away with three golden takeaways that every embedded engineer and CTF author should tattoo on their forearm:


1. Hardware Security Has No "Undo" (Unless It's Planned by Design)


When you author CTF challenges on your own personal hardware, remember that the bootloader does not know you are "just practicing." If your code says burn eFuse, it will burn that fuse with remorseless silicon finality. My JTAG pins (DIS_PAD_JTAG, DIS_USB_JTAG) are gone forever. No software trick in the world will ever bring them back.


2. Parity Bits Are a Developer's Best Friend


The fact that Espressif designed SPI_BOOT_CRYPT_CNT as an odd-even parity counter instead of a 1-bit boolean switch is an absolute masterclass in developer empathy. They knew people like me would accidentally lock their boards during testing, and they built a mathematical escape route directly into the silicon logic.


3. I Have Exactly Zero Lives Left


My board's SPI_BOOT_CRYPT_CNT is currently 0b011 (value 3). If I ever accidentally enable Flash Encryption on this board again, the counter will transition to 0b111 (value 7).


At that point, all three bits will be 1. There are no more bits to burn. There will be no even parity. There will be no redemption.


So if you see me reaching for --commit on this ESP32-S3 ever again... please slap the keyboard out of my hands.


Happy hacking, and may your eFuses stay unburned!
